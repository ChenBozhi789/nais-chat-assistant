## Page 9

### Better prompts = better answers

Watch how the same topic improves as the prompt gets more specific. Try this with photosynthesis — or any topic from class.
LEVEL 1
Broad
PROMPT
"What is photosynthesis?"
WHAT YO U GET
You get a textbook-style definition. Useful, but generic.
LEVEL 2
Focused
PROMPT
"Explain the most important steps of photosynthesis and how it helps
plants grow."
WHAT YO U GET
Now the answer focuses on the steps and the growth link —
what you actually need.
LEVEL 3
Tuned to you
PROMPT
"Explain the most important steps of photosynthesis and how it helps
plants grow. Pitch it for a 14-year-old."
WHAT YO U GET
Plain language, simpler examples. Easier to learn from and
remember.

## Page 10

### A simple prompt recipe

Mix these five ingredients and your prompts level up. You don't need them all every time — but the more you add, the more useful
the response.
R
Role
Tell it who to be. 'Act like my history tutor.'
T
Task
Say exactly what you want. 'Explain', 'compare', 'summarise', 'plan'.
C
Context
What's the subject? Year level? Word limit? Audience?
E
Examples
Give a sample or template if you have one.
F
Format
Ask for bullets, a table, an outline, or a paragraph.
ALL FIVE IN ONE
PROMPT
"Act like my Year 11 history
tutor (R). Outline (T) a 600-
word persuasive essay on
whether the Treaty of Waitangi
was fairly translated (C). Use
my three points: language
differences, intent, and
consequences. Format as
introduction → three body
paragraphs → conclusion (F)."

## Page 12

### When AI sounds confident but is wrong

Large language models generate words that look plausible. Sometimes the words happen to be wrong — fake quotes, invented
sources, made-up dates. This is called a hallucination. It happens to every model.
Common slip-ups
- Invented citations — book titles or authors that don't exist
- Broken or made-up URLs
- Wrong dates, statistics, or percentages
- Confident-sounding answers in topics where it has thin training
data
- Mixing real facts with subtle errors
How to verify
- Ask the model to cite its sources, then check them
- Cross-check with Gemini or Copilot (both search the live web)
- Use Perplexity — it shows sources for every claim
- Verify facts in your textbook, library, or an .edu / .govt.nz site
- If you can't verify it, don't put it in your assignment

## Page 14

### Trust, but verify

Try this exercise. Ask any chatbot: "Give me 5 sources on the Battle of Crete in WW2 — at least three accessible online." Then
check the sources.
1
Copy each URL into your browser
Does the page exist? Is it the right page?
2
Check book titles in your library catalogue
Te Puna, your school library, or WorldCat.
3
Match author names against the publisher
Does the publisher list the same book under that author?
4
Compare claims against a second source
If two sources disagree, dig deeper.
5
Cite the source, not the chatbot
Reference the book/website you actually used.

### Upload a PDF, then ask questions

All four chatbots accept document uploads in 2026 — PDFs, Word docs, slides, even images. Click the paperclip in the prompt box,

Try these on your next reading
Summarise "Give me a one-page summary of this article in plain English."

### Perplexity — answers with citations

Perplexity is a research-first chatbot. Every claim has a numbered source you can click through to. Great when you need to cite
what you find.
Web search
Type a question — Perplexity searches the
live web and gives a written answer with
footnoted sources.
Summarise pages
Install the browser extension — open any
article and click 'Summarise' for an instant
overview.
Focus the search
Narrow Perplexity to just this page, just this
domain, or the whole web — perfect for
trustworthy sites.
Try this
Open a long article you've been assigned. Click the Perplexity extension. Click 'Summarise'. Then ask a follow-up like "What was the main cause
discussed?" or "Who is the author quoting?". Set the focus to just this page so the answer stays grounded in your reading.
